Floor runner: `pdfplumber`. Coverage is measured against it, so its own coverage row is 1.0 by definition.

No column is a quality score and no column should be averaged with another. When `arith checked` is nonzero, read `arith pass` first — it is the only column that can be flatly wrong.

Caveat: on this corpus, `arith checked` reads 0 across all runners. These papers have no whole-cell total labels — a cell that is just `Total`, `Sum`, or `Overall`. Candidates such as `Total GWP 100` exist but are quantity names, not aggregate labels, and are not matched by design (matching them would silently sum unrelated columns beside them). This is not a failure of any runner; it is the honest outcome of a label-free metric on a corpus without checkable totals. The measurement weight falls to coverage and cross-runner agreement.

Caveat: `order monotonic` still is not evidence of correct reading order, but it is no longer tautological for every runner. `pdfplumber` and `pdfjs-node` bucket lines by rounded top before emitting them, so their blocks are geometrically sorted by construction and this always reads 1.000 for them — asking whether sorted output is sorted always answers yes. `docling-default` and `docling-tuned` now keep docling's own `texts[]` order instead of being re-sorted geometrically (see Findings), so this column measures something real for them for the first time, and it is below 1.000 (0.919 / 0.960). Spot-checked on `A. Priyadarsini et al. 2023`: the backward jumps there are not clean two-column breaks — they are page furniture (footers, stray page-number glyphs) sitting between body text in `texts[]`, and this runner's own table-after-text placement (a table's visual position can be near the top of a page it is read after in full). `midword` remains the weaker, but real, per-block signal.

Caveat: `sections/6` and the chunk columns compare within a runner family, not across. The docling runners supply their own `section_header` labels, while `pdfplumber` and `pdfjs-node` route all text through a font-blind classifier. Measured on one paper: docling found 5 of 6 canonical sections, pdfplumber 3, pdfjs 2 — reading that as "docling detects structure better" would be an artifact of the classifier, not a measurement.

Caveat: `dropped pages` reads 0 for every runner on this corpus, and that is a fix, not a finding. ## Findings

**A counting bug reversed the totals.** `chars_per_page` and `page_text` used to count only `paragraph`/`section_header`/`caption`/`other` blocks. A table block always carries `text: ""` — its content lives in `tables[].cells` instead — so every page a runner tabulated read as a page it had lost content on. All 15 of `docling-default`'s "dropped pages" and all 21 of `docling-tuned`'s were table pages, not losses, and that was the entire dropped-pages story in the previous version of this report; it is retracted, not refined. With table cells counted like-for-like, `dropped pages` reads 0 for all four runners on this corpus (see the caveat above), and the corpus-wide character totals invert: `docling-default` now leads. Known side effect, stated rather than fixed: `pdfplumber`'s own `extract_words` already includes words that sit inside its 79 tables, so counting those tables' cells on top double-counts 25,324 of its characters (about 1.2% of its total). That means the floor is itself very slightly inflated, and every other runner's ratio-to-floor in this report reads roughly 1% more conservative than it should.

**The hosting question.** On the full 47-paper corpus, total characters extracted (table cells included) are: `docling-default` 2,321,204 (1.047x the floor), `pdfjs-node` 2,265,452 (1.022x), `docling-tuned` 2,218,401 (1.001x), `pdfplumber` (floor) 2,216,819. `docling-default` leads on the corpus-wide total; `pdfjs-node` is second and clearly ahead of `docling-tuned`, which is now flat against the floor rather than either winning or losing meaningfully. The mean-of-per-paper-medians column agrees on the ordering (`docling-default` 1.052, `pdfjs-node` 1.039, `docling-tuned` 1.009, `pdfplumber` 1.000). Cross-runner text agreement puts `pdfjs-node` closer to the floor (`pdfjs-node` vs `pdfplumber` = 0.842) than `docling-default` is (`docling-default` vs `pdfplumber` = 0.793), which is expected — `pdfjs-node` and `pdfplumber` both read the embedded text layer directly, while both docling runners run a layout/OCR pipeline on top of it. On speed, `pdfjs-node` ran the full corpus at 0.04s/page against `docling-tuned`'s measured 730s / 602 pages = 1.21s/page (about 30x faster). On tables the answer is separate and blunt: `pdfjs-node` produced zero tables on all 47 papers, by construction, against 129 (`docling-default`), 128 (`docling-tuned`), and 79 (`pdfplumber`'s own naive detector). There is also a tail risk this corpus-wide comparison does not weight by frequency: on this corpus's one scanned paper, `Xin et al. 2004` (6 pages, no embedded text layer), `pdfplumber` recovers 0 characters and `pdfjs-node` recovers 0 characters, while `docling-default` recovers 18,471 and `docling-tuned` 18,630. A browser-side, zero-server design that reads only the embedded text layer — which is what shipping `pdfjs-node` alone would mean — returns nothing at all on a scanned upload, silently, and with no server there is no OCR fallback to catch it. This is low-frequency here (one paper in 47) and total when it happens, distinct from the coverage and table questions above: it is a shipping decision the hosting question has to weigh on its own terms, not evidence that a zero-server design is unworkable — 46 of 47 papers in this corpus are born-digital and `pdfjs-node` handles those fine.

That character-recovery lead for `pdfjs-node` needs one large qualification, which no other metric in this lab used to catch: **column interleaving.** This is no longer hand-computed prose — it is `lab/metrics.column_interleaving`, wired into `score()`, regenerated by every `compare`, and covered by three tests. It counts the share of `paragraph` blocks whose bbox spans a page's likely column gutter, using thresholds that are fractions of that page's own extent (`x0 < 0.35 * extent` and `x1 > 0.65 * extent`, extent = the runner's own max block x1 on that page) rather than the fixed points (`x0 < 200`, `x1 > 400`) the first pass used — page width on this corpus runs from about 386pt to about 1329pt, and the same PDF yields a different max-x1 per runner, so absolute points were never comparable across papers or runners. Two independent effects matter here. First, granularity: median paragraph length is 192 chars (`pdfplumber`) and 188 (`pdfjs-node`) against 17 (`docling-default`) and 15 (`docling-tuned`) — most docling blocks are structurally too short to physically span a gutter, regardless of interleaving. Measured over every paragraph block, corpus-wide: `pdfplumber` 75.0% (2,279 of 3,037), `pdfjs-node` 70.0% (2,141 of 3,059), `docling-default` 11.8% (1,362 of 11,494), `docling-tuned` 10.7% (1,248 of 11,612). Size-matched to blocks of 150+ characters — the `gutter-span (150ch+)` column in the table above, pooled by summing spanning and total blocks across all 47 papers rather than averaging per-paper ratios — the honest comparison is: `pdfplumber` 96.3% (1,599 of 1,661), `pdfjs-node` 88.9% (1,461 of 1,643), `docling-default` 26.3% (985 of 3,752), `docling-tuned` 26.6% (904 of 3,396). The gap between the line-based runners and docling narrows from roughly 7x on all blocks to roughly 4x once size is matched — still a clear docling win on this axis, but honestly sized rather than inflated by counting thousands of docling fragments too short to ever cross a gutter. Say this plainly: on a two-column page, `pdfjs-node` recovers the characters but delivers a large share of them (89% of its substantial paragraphs) in an order a RAG chunk cannot use, because the line-bucketing both line-based runners share has no notion of a column boundary. This does not make `pdfjs-node` unusable — the ratio is page-layout-dependent, not a property of the tool, and a single-column PDF would not show it; a genuinely full-width paragraph (an abstract, a wide table caption) also counts as spanning, which is a known ceiling of this metric, not a defect in either runner. But it does mean the character-count lead over `docling-tuned` cannot be read as an unqualified coverage win without also weighing that most of that runner's substantial paragraphs on this corpus are column-merged. No metric elsewhere in this report — not coverage, not agreement, not `order monotonic` — would have surfaced this; all three would have scored a cleanly merged two-column paragraph as ordinary recovered text.

**Does `docling-tuned` beat `docling-default`?** No, on every axis measured. Coverage: total characters dropped from 2,321,204 to 2,218,401, a corpus-wide loss of 102,803 characters (-4.43%); the mean-of-medians column drops from 1.052 to 1.009 in the same direction. The brief also asked about table arithmetic specifically, but that check is silent for both runners on this corpus — `arith checked` reads 0 across the board (see the caveat above), so it cannot separate them at all. Table count is what is actually comparable instead, and it barely moved (129 vs 128), so tuning bought nothing there either. Broken out per paper: 43 of 47 papers lost characters under forced full-page OCR and only 4 gained. The single manifest-flagged scanned paper, `Xin et al. 2004`, gained 159 characters (+0.9%) — OCR is exactly what a scanned page needs. The other three gains were small (`Jiang et al. 2023` +189/+0.4%, `Chen et al. 2021` +519/+1.2%, `Pontes et al 2009` +1,283/+3.7%). The worst born-digital losses were `Kim et al. 2021 - Biogas from Wastewater Sludge` (-16,626, -24.7%), `Patel et al. 2023` (-10,161, -18.4%), `Sahoo et al. 2022` (-5,289, -8.2%), `Sahoo et al. 2023` (-4,911, -7.3%), and `Joseph et al. 2022` (-4,784, -6.4%). The direction of this finding is not in doubt — a sign test on 43 losses of 47 papers gives p of roughly 1.4e-9 — but the magnitude is concentrated: those five worst-losing papers account for 41,771 of the corpus's 104,953 total lost characters, about 39.8%. Dropping the single worst, `Kim et al. 2021 - Biogas from Wastewater Sludge` (-16,626), from both sides of the corpus total moves the figure from -4.43% (102,803 of 2,321,204) to about -3.8% (86,177 of 2,253,958 over the remaining 46 papers) — characterizing how much of the headline number rides on one paper, not weakening the finding itself. Cost: summing each paper's own `wall_seconds`, the full `docling-tuned` run took 730.2s over 602 pages (1.21s/page, ~12.2 minutes); the report's mean-of-per-paper-ratios column reads 1.37s/page instead — the same pooled-vs-mean-of-ratios gap already flagged for the empty-cells column, not two independent measurements disagreeing. Either figure says the same thing: that cost bought a worse result than the free default. One caveat on comparability: this is not a perfectly controlled A/B. `docling-default`'s numbers are v1's actual historical output, imported from JSON produced in v1's environment; `docling-tuned` is a fresh conversion run on this machine. The reproduction check below found that an environment-matched fresh `docling-default` run agrees with that imported baseline to within about 0.05% of total characters. Against a measured tuning effect of 4.43%, an 0.05% cross-environment drift is roughly two orders of magnitude smaller — nowhere near large enough to be the source of the loss. The conclusion holds: full-page OCR does not beat an embedded text layer here, and it actively hurts on born-digital PDFs, which are the overwhelming majority of this corpus (46 of 47 papers by the manifest's scanned-page heuristic). One further scope limit, not a finding: `force_full_page_ocr` resolves to whichever OCR engine `OcrAutoOptions` picks on the machine that runs it — `ocrmac` (Apple Vision) here (`runner_version` now records this as `docling==2.60.0+ocrmac`), but RapidOCR on Linux, with different text and much slower timings. "OCR hurts on born-digital pages" is an Apple-Vision-specific finding until someone reruns this on Linux and shows otherwise.

**There was almost no tuning available.** This needs a correction too: docling exposes real knobs — `TableFormerMode.FAST` vs `ACCURATE`, which OCR engine to force, `images_scale`, and others — so "there was one knob" overstated the case. The accurate claim is narrower: on docling 2.60.0, every pipeline option the plan proposed tuning (`do_ocr`, `do_table_structure`, `TableFormerMode.ACCURATE`, `do_cell_matching`, `generate_page_images=False`) was already the library default, so `docling-tuned` turning them "on" changed nothing. The one variable it genuinely adds over `docling-default` is `force_full_page_ocr=True` (the explicit `DoclingParseV4DocumentBackend` is also today's default; it is pinned for version-bump protection, not because it changes behavior now), and turning that on made results worse on 43 of 47 papers.

**Where all four runners disagree.** Excluding `Xin et al. 2004` (the 6-page scan whose non-OCR runners legitimately see 0 characters, which trivially produces 0.000 agreement against them), the paper with the lowest agreement across every runner pair is `Patel et al. 2023` (minimum pairwise per-paper-median agreement 0.456, mean across pairs 0.720); it is also independently `docling-tuned`'s single worst-coverage paper (0.809). Close behind: `Hamilton et al. 2024` (0.556) and `Jovanovic et al. 2021 (part I)` (0.564) — the latter also lands in the worst-five coverage list for three of the four runners independently (`docling-default` 0.833, `docling-tuned` 0.86, `pdfjs-node` 0.94), the strongest corroborated case of a genuinely hard PDF in this corpus. These three papers are worth opening by eye before trusting any single runner's output for them.

**What this lab cannot tell you.** There are no ground-truth labels anywhere in this corpus, so nothing above measures absolute correctness — only relative agreement and relative coverage against an arbitrary floor runner. `arith checked` reads 0 for every runner on all 47 papers: this corpus has no whole-cell total labels; candidates such as `Total GWP 100` exist but are quantity names, not aggregate labels, and are not matched by design, so the table-arithmetic metric found nothing to check here rather than everyone getting it right. `order monotonic` reads 1.000 for `pdfplumber` and `pdfjs-node` for a structural reason, not a quality one — both bucket lines into geometric order before emitting them, so the check answers "is sorted output sorted," which is always yes. It is no longer tautological for the docling runners: now that they keep docling's own `texts[]` reading order instead of being re-sorted by position (see the caveat above), `order monotonic` reads 0.919 (`docling-default`) and 0.960 (`docling-tuned`) — a real signal, though spot-checking it on `A. Priyadarsini et al. 2023` found the backward jumps were mostly page furniture (footers, stray page-number glyphs) and this runner's own table-after-text placement, not clean two-column breaks. `midword` remains the column that varies most informatively. Finally, the structural-hash reproduction check tested exact structural equality — identical blocks and tables, field for field — against a fresh `docling-default` run on the same three PDFs, and it came back MISMATCH on all three: `A. Priyadarsini et al. 2023` (blocks 242 to 241, 0.4%; characters 48,938 to 48,937, 0.002%; tables 3 to 3, unchanged), `Adegbola thesis High Density Cultures` (blocks 1245 to 1236, 0.72%; characters 91,349 to 91,306, 0.047%; tables 17 to 17, unchanged), and `Ahmadi & Lackner 2024` (blocks 415 to 399, 3.86%; characters 81,932 to 81,941, 0.011%; tables 6 to 6, unchanged). That is a genuine failure of the criterion, not a false alarm — the check asked for byte-for-byte equality and did not get it. But the criterion was stricter than the question it meant to ask: text content agrees to within 0.05% on every paper tested, table counts agree exactly on all three, and only block segmentation differs, by 0.4% to 3.9%. The practical consequence is that `docling-default`'s numbers in this report are v1's actual historical output, and a fresh docling 2.60.0 install here reproduces them in substance — same text, same tables — but not byte-for-byte in how that text is split into blocks. (Block order was not part of this equality check and is unaffected by which sort key `ir_from_docling_dict` uses — reordering the same blocks does not change how many there are or what they say.)
